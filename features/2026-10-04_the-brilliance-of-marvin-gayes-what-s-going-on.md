# The Brilliance of Marvin Gaye’s ‘What’s Going On’

> *How a studio accident and a refusal to follow the Motown rules created the most hauntingly beautiful record in soul history.*

Within the initial six seconds of *What’s Going On*, the composition refuses the sterility of the traditional studio booth. Instead, it captures a living, breathing social geography.

The clinking of glass and the localized chatter of Detroit Lions players Lem Barney and Mel Farr function as more than just “background noise.” They serve as a spatial anchor, a low-pass filtered ambient bed that prevents the ethereal Emaj7(add9) opening chord from drifting into pure abstraction.

As Eli Fontaine’s saxophone skims across this social surface — captured in a single, spontaneous warm-up take — its reedy, uncompressed grit punctures the lushness of the Funk Brothers’ arrangement. This is the sound of a masterpiece finding its footing in real time.

### The Song at a Glance

- **Artist:** Marvin Gaye
- **Album:** *What’s Going On* (1971)
- **Key:** E Major / C# Minor (utilizing significant Dorian and Mixolydian inflections)
- **Tempo:** 101 BPM
- **Structure:** Iterative through-composed rondo with an extended atmospheric vamp

### Structural Excavation: The Gravity of the Groove

The harmonic foundation of the track rests on a sophisticated interplay between James Jamerson’s syncopated bass motility and the Detroit Symphony Orchestra’s suspended string voicing.

Jamerson, a titan of the low end, famously avoids the tonic downbeat here. Instead, he opts for a chromatic approach that skirts the edges of the Emaj7 and C#m9 chords. This creates a “suspended” architecture; the song never truly sits on its heels, maintaining a constant sense of forward lean.

The rhythmic friction arises from the conga patterns of Eddie “Bongo” Brown. These operate in a subdivided triplet feel against the straight sixteenth-note hi-hat pulse, creating a microscopic polyrhythmic tension — a “swing” that feels heavy, like mercury moving through a glass tube.

### Echoes of Time and the Fractured Machine

*What’s Going On* marks the precise moment where the Motown “Assembly Line” fractured, allowing the auteur to emerge from the machine. It is a sonic response to the escalating friction of the Vietnam War and the domestic erosion of the American Dream. The track functions as a funeral oration for the 1960s, delivered not from a pulpit, but from the street corner.

### The Musical Secret: The Myth of the “Self-Sample”

The prevailing myth suggests that Gaye’s haunting, multi-layered vocal was a calculated “sampling” of his own voice to create a choir. The musicological reality is far more chaotic and brilliant: it was an accidental heterophony.

During the mix-down, engineer Ken Sands played two separate lead vocal takes simultaneously — one “soulful” and one “refined” — intending for Gaye to choose between them. The resulting phase-interference and micro-temporal offsets created a “third voice.”

This wasn’t a sample; it was a temporal collision. Because the two takes possessed slight pitch variances (within 5–10 cents), they generated a natural “chorusing” effect that electronic oscillators simply cannot replicate. The song’s power lies in Gaye’s refusal to resolve his own internal duality. He is literally arguing with himself across the stereo field, creating a spectral frequency mask that gives the vocal its ghostly translucence.

> “Marvin had a way of just… leaning against the mic. He wasn’t singing to the balcony; he was whispering to the person standing right behind your eyes.” — *Anonymous Motown Session Hand*

### Notation of the Invisible

If you were to look at the lead sheet for this track, it would be a forest of accidentals. The transition from the A-section to the bridge involves a shift to an F#7(13) that refuses to resolve to B7, instead sliding back into the Emaj7.

This harmonic circularity mimics the lyrical themes of cycles and systemic stagnation. Meanwhile, the strings are scored in high-register clusters, creating a “shimmer” that sits above the 5 kHz range. This leaves the midrange entirely open for the vocal combat taking place between Gaye’s two personas.

### Afterglow

The track does not end so much as it evaporates. It leaves the listener to inhabit the silence it leaves behind — a silence now heavy with the weight of the questions it posed over fifty years ago. In the world of *What’s Going On*, the music doesn’t provide the answers; it simply provides the space to ask them.
